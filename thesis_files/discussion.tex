\section{Discussion and Conclusion} 
\subsection{Discussion}
The results presented in Chapter~\ref{sec:results} demonstrated that the
prototype supports portable identity, structured social actions,
pull-based replication, account discovery and feed publication. These
results provide evidence that the requirements identified in Chapter~3
can be realised through the Git-native protocol proposed in
Chapter~\ref{sec:system-design}.

The following sections discuss the major design decisions of the
protocol, compare them with existing approaches, and examine the
trade-offs revealed through implementation and evaluation.

\subsubsection*{Identity and Portability}

A key outcome of the implemented protocol is the separation of identity
from repository location. As described in the Identity Model presented
in Chapter~\ref{sec:system-design}, identity is defined by an Ed25519
public key rather than a repository URL or hosting provider. A user's
public key therefore acts as the authoritative identifier, while
repositories function solely as storage and distribution mechanisms.

The identity-creation and publication workflows presented in
Chapter~\ref{sec:results} demonstrated that repository URLs are treated
as metadata associated with a signed identity rather than forming part of
the identity itself. This distinction allows repositories to be
relocated without requiring the creation of a new account or social
identity.

Compared with ActivityPub, this provides a stronger notion of identity
portability. ActivityPub identities are generally associated with server
domains and therefore remain dependent on the continued operation and
policies of a particular server. Similarly, Octotown inherits GitHub's
account model and ties identity directly to a GitHub username. In
contrast, the implemented protocol is more closely aligned with Nostr,
where public keys rather than infrastructure determine identity
ownership. The design also shares similarities with Social4Git, which
demonstrated that repositories can move between providers, although
Social4Git lacked a portable cryptographic identity layer.

The protocol supplements cryptographic identities with human-readable
handles. This approach is conceptually similar to the handle-based layer
used by AT Protocol, where a user-friendly identifier is mapped to a
portable cryptographic identity. The directory therefore improves
discoverability without changing the underlying identity model.

The results also highlighted an important trade-off. Public-key-based
identities provide portability and independence from hosting providers,
but they are not convenient for human users. Meaningful discovery
requires a mechanism for associating identities with user-friendly
handles. The repository-replacement scenario demonstrated that digital
signatures alone are insufficient for identity continuity; continuity
also depends on a trusted record linking a handle to its previously
registered public key. Thus, while the protocol reduces dependence on
hosting providers, it introduces a limited trust dependency in the
discovery layer.

\subsubsection*{Replication and Distribution}

The implemented protocol adopts a pull-based replication model built on
Git's native synchronisation mechanisms. Rather than distributing
updates through server-to-server delivery, repositories explicitly
retrieve content from configured remotes and locally verify it before
acceptance.

The literature review highlighted several limitations in alternative
distribution approaches. ActivityPub relies on push-based fan-out, where
servers are responsible for delivering activities to followers.
Although effective, this design places increasing load on servers as the
number of followers grows. Nostr uses relays to forward events but
provides weaker guarantees regarding state management and ordering.
Secure Scuttlebutt removes servers entirely through peer-to-peer gossip,
but replication becomes increasingly expensive as message histories
grow.

The implemented design instead reuses Git's pull-based replication
model, as demonstrated by Gitweets, Social4Git and GitSocial. However,
the protocol extends these systems by replicating verified protocol
actions rather than treating Git history itself as the social feed. Git
is therefore reused as a transport and distribution mechanism, while
protocol-level validation remains the responsibility of the client.

The replication and interaction workflows presented in
Chapter~\ref{sec:results} demonstrated that signed actions can be
fetched, verified and accepted using only standard Git operations. This
addresses one of the key shortcomings identified in the survey, namely
the reliance on specialised replication infrastructure while still
maintaining structured social interactions.

A particularly interesting outcome of the implementation was the
emergence of multi-hop replication. The results showed that Alice was
able to receive Charlie's post through Bob, despite not directly
following Charlie. This behaviour enables social content to spread
beyond immediate follow relationships while preserving authenticity.

Unlike simple repository mirroring, trust does not move to the
repository distributing the content. Verification continues to use the
original author's public key, meaning that intermediate repositories act
as transport mechanisms rather than trust anchors. The multi-hop
replication scenario therefore demonstrates that repository-centric
distribution can support decentralised content propagation without
requiring trusted intermediaries.

More broadly, the results suggest that Git's repository and remote model
can provide an effective foundation for social-data exchange. Existing
Git-based systems demonstrated pull-based replication, but the current
implementation extends this concept with cryptographic verification and
structured social actions, producing a more complete replication model.

\subsubsection*{Discovery and Usability}

Discovery emerged as one of the most significant gaps identified during
the protocol survey. ActivityPub provides discovery through WebFinger
and server infrastructure, while AT Protocol relies on DID and
DNS-based services. Nostr provides portable identities but lacks a
widely adopted built-in discovery mechanism. Most Git-based systems
require repository URLs to be exchanged manually before any interaction
can occur.

To address this limitation, the implemented protocol introduces a public
social directory that maps handles to repository URLs and public keys.
This allows users to discover accounts through familiar identifiers
while preserving cryptographic verification during the Follow workflow.
The directory therefore serves as a usability layer rather than a source
of authenticity, with signatures continuing to provide authorship and
integrity guarantees.

The discovery and follow workflows presented in
Chapter~\ref{sec:results} demonstrated that this approach significantly
reduces the need for prior repository-URL exchange compared with
Gitweets, Social4Git and GitSocial. Instead of requiring users to know a
repository URL in advance, accounts can be located through a
human-readable handle and then validated against the corresponding
directory entry.

The implementation also introduced static-site publication through
GitHub Pages. The generated websites provide browser-accessible feeds
displaying profile information, posts, replies and likes. Unlike the
protocol itself, which operates through Git repositories and JSON
actions, these websites exist purely as presentation layers. Feed
publication therefore improves accessibility without altering the
underlying protocol workflows.

An important observation from the implementation is that usability
improvements frequently introduce additional trust assumptions. The
social directory significantly improves discoverability, and
browser-based feeds improve accessibility, but neither feature is
strictly required for protocol operation. The protocol can function
entirely through Git repositories and cryptographic identities. This
reinforces a broader distinction between the core protocol and
supporting infrastructure: usability services can be layered on top of
the protocol without becoming part of the protocol's trust model.

Compared with earlier Git-based systems, this represents a substantial
improvement in usability. Gitweets exposes Git operations directly to
users, while Social4Git and GitSocial still require users to know
repository URLs and invoke protocol-specific commands. The browser-based
directory and feed publication workflows developed in this project make
the protocol more accessible without changing the underlying
architecture.

Nevertheless, the prototype remains primarily command-line oriented.
Users still perform operations such as following, replication and
publication through explicit commands. While this provides a higher
level of abstraction than systems which expose native Git commands
directly, it does not yet offer the level of accessibility expected of
mainstream social-networking applications.

\subsubsection*{Social Vocabulary and Data Representation}

The protocol represents social interactions through a structured
vocabulary consisting of Posts, Replies, Likes and Follows. This design
was informed by the survey of existing systems. ActivityPub
demonstrated the value of modelling social interactions through
explicit activity types, while Nostr similarly distinguishes different
social actions through event kinds.

Unlike Gitweets and GitSocial, which embed social content within commit
messages, the implemented protocol stores social actions as independent
JSON objects. This allows metadata such as authorship, timestamps,
signatures and references to be represented explicitly rather than
inferred from Git history. The resulting structure is easier to
validate, process and extend across different implementations.

The action creation and interaction workflows presented in
Chapter~\ref{sec:results} demonstrated the use of a common action
structure across Posts, Replies, Likes and Follows. These relationships
were subsequently reconstructed during feed construction without relying
on provider-specific functionality. This separation of creation,
replication and presentation produced a cleaner architecture than
approaches that couple social activity directly to version-control
operations.

The use of JSON also provides extensibility. The protocol currently
supports only four action types, but the common action structure allows
new social interactions to be introduced without altering the existing
representation. The recent discussion regarding Boost or Retweet actions
provides a useful example: such an action could be introduced as an
additional JSON object referencing an existing post while preserving the
existing protocol architecture.

This demonstrates one of the key benefits of structured social data.
Earlier Git-based systems often used commit messages or
provider-specific features to represent interactions, limiting
flexibility and interoperability. By contrast, the implemented protocol
separates social activity from repository history and stores it using a
consistent machine-readable format. The result is a social vocabulary
that is easier to validate, extend and implement across different
clients.

\subsubsection*{Overall Assessment}

The protocol was designed to combine strengths identified across both
Git-based and non-Git-based decentralised social systems while
addressing several recurring limitations identified during the
literature review.

From Nostr and AT Protocol, it adopts portable cryptographic identity.
From ActivityPub and Nostr, it adopts the concept of structured social
actions. From Gitweets, Social4Git and GitSocial, it adopts
repository-centric storage and pull-based replication. These concepts
were successfully integrated into a single Git-native architecture
without requiring a central platform for social-data exchange.

The results presented in Chapter~\ref{sec:results} indicate that this
approach is feasible in practice. The prototype successfully
demonstrated portable identity, structured JSON actions, pull-based
replication, discovery through human-readable handles, multi-hop
content propagation and browser-accessible static feeds. Collectively,
these capabilities address many of the limitations identified in the
survey of existing protocols and subsequently formalised as requirements
in Chapter~3.

However, the implementation also revealed that some challenges are
transformed rather than eliminated. Identity portability reduces
dependence on hosting providers but increases reliance on trusted
handle-to-key mappings. Pull-based replication reduces infrastructure
requirements but introduces replication latency. Browser-based feed
publication improves accessibility but leaves core protocol operations
dependent on a command-line client. These trade-offs suggest that
decentralisation should not be viewed as the removal of all
dependencies, but rather as a redistribution of trust and
responsibility within the system.
